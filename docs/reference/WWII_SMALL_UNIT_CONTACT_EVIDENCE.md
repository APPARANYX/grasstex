# WWII Small-Unit Contact: Firsthand Evidence and Special-Operations Theory

> **Research reference for GRASSTEX, 2026-10-26.** Historical interpretation and proposed *game-simulation* evaluations, **not** an implemented gameplay contract, a prescriptive real-world field manual, or a source of calibrated gameplay constants.
>
> **Scope:** What American infantrymen and observers described during the first moments of contact with German forces in open/approach terrain, woodland, bocage and streets; how that differs from planned assaults; and which concepts from William H. McRaven's *Spec Ops* can inform a WWII simulation.
>
> **Evidence boundary:** Directly accessible veteran interviews, memoir passages, and Ernie Pyle columns were read; McRaven's *Spec Ops* is represented through publisher descriptions, bibliographic previews and independent military histories of its WWII case studies. **The full copyrighted book was not independently read**; do not attribute unverified page-specific claims or quotation to McRaven.

## Executive findings

1. **Two kinds of first contact must not be collapsed.** In a chance encounter, neither party may understand the other side's dispositions or intentions. In an ambush or prepared defense, the waiting side may inflict rapid casualties before the moving side can identify firing positions. [S1] [S2] [S4]
2. **The opening reaction need not be a synchronized squad action.** Soldiers fire, drop, freeze, seek cover, or comply with immediate leader directions at different times. Coherent squad coordination may develop *after* initial contact. [S1] [S2] [S4] [S5]
3. **Firing at a suspected position is distinct from aimed fire on an identified person.** In the Normandy hedgerows, Pyle reported Americans shooting into concealed defensive hedges to keep defenders down while small groups advanced. This supports a meaningful distinction between suppressing a sector and scoring a precise hit without current target visibility. [S6]
4. **Terrain and exposure shape tempo.** Pyle described short moves and pauses around hedgerows and single-man crossings at an exposed urban culvert; another veteran described a platoon struck as it crested a rise. Movement can be cautious without a universal ten-second inactivity delay. [S4] [S5] [S6]
5. **A local success can create a new crisis.** An American patrol-contact victory near Nijmegen led to overextension and temporary isolation. Contact resolution should not automatically mean objective security or permission to pursue indefinitely. [S3]
6. **McRaven's relative superiority is a useful lens for *planned* attacks, not a universal chance-contact algorithm.** His six named principles are simplicity, security, repetition, surprise, speed and purpose. Surprise and local initiative can produce a temporary advantage that must be exploited, but a rehearsed special operation has preconditions an accidental two-squad collision does not. [S7] [S8] [S9] [S10]

## Method and evidence confidence

- **First-person testimony:** directly attributed participant recollection (Ehlers, Barnhart, Romero, Kotlowitz); often recorded decades after the event, vulnerable to retrospective memory and selection effects.
- **Contemporary observation:** Ernie Pyle's 1944 columns; better time proximity, but still qualitative reportage and often at company/platoon scale.
- **Later study:** McRaven's theory and operational case studies; focused on planned special operations, not statistics for ordinary U.S.–German meeting engagements.
- **Simulation inference:** a plausible design or test suggested by the historical evidence, **not** something the source established as a game mechanic.
- No representative sample of engagements, per-round hit rates, universal reaction-time distributions, bullet flight times, or measured suppression coefficients is supplied here. Do **not** use isolated accounts to fabricate any of these values.

## Encounter dossiers: what happened at contact

### E1 — A rapid, face-to-face hedge encounter: Walter D. Ehlers, Normandy, 9 June 1944

**Situation (reported):** After Americans ahead were fired upon, Staff Sgt. Walter Ehlers moved toward a hedgerow with his men. Hearing movement and reaching the hedge bank, he found himself facing four armed German patrol members at very short distance. He recalled immediately firing four M1 shots, hitting all four, reloading, and directing his men to fix bayonets before continuing into further German positions. This is Ehlers's *own report*, not a measured representative probability of four successive hits. [S1]

**What the evidence supports:** A highly experienced, alert individual could seize the initiative at very short range before formal squad re-tasking. Surprise did not mechanically prevent immediate individual action.

**What it does not support:** Equal rapid reactions for all troops, universally reliable snap shooting, or instant recognition of every opposing combatant.

**GRASSTEX evaluation idea:** In extremely close mutual contact, instrument the order of detection, weapon readiness, first shot, first received shot, alert propagation and squad-command adoption. Permit individual reflex action without granting instantaneous shared enemy truth.

### E2 — Ambiguous first shots in forest contact: Lt. Robert M. Barnhart, eastern France, autumn 1944

**Situation (reported):** Newly arrived Lt. Robert M. Barnhart, 179th Infantry, 45th Division, asked veteran platoon sergeant Lew Eaves to help him learn the unit. On the platoon's third day out, while sweeping woodland, the Americans unexpectedly met Germans. The exchange lasted only a few minutes, ending when 14 Germans surrendered. One wounded prisoner complained that the Germans had wanted to surrender before the shooting; Barnhart did not know which side had fired first. Eaves was hit during the firefight and was lost to the platoon. Barnhart separately described the BAR gunner Wilson firing bursts. [S2]

**What the evidence supports:** Disputed or uncertain initiation, intense local automatic fire, real consequences for a vulnerable leader, and rapid termination other than total annihilation.

**What it does not support:** A verified second-by-second timeline, certainty about German intent, or a universal propensity to surrender.

**GRASSTEX evaluation idea:** Track who *believed* contact began first separately from who actually fired first; distinguish visible opponent, probable firing sector, and unknown contact. A leader casualty should affect communication/coordination, not silently erase all soldier autonomy.

### E3 — A local win turns into isolation: Pvt. Angel Romero, Devil's Hill near Nijmegen, September 1944

**Situation (reported):** During the fighting for Devil's Hill, a German patrol ran into Company A's left flank. A U.S. paratrooper squad drove the patrol off but pursued/advanced into German-held ground and became cut off in a ravine. Romero recalled a discussion of escape or surrender; a German-speaking man exchanged words with the enemy, shooting resumed, and the Americans attempted to break out. This is an eyewitness passage reproduced within a later operational narrative. [S3]

**What the evidence supports:** First-contact success can lead to exposure, flank danger and changed circumstances. A squad may need to disengage from a locally favorable fight to survive a deteriorating position.

**What it does not support:** A generic preference for pursuit or an exact frequency of isolation after contact.

**GRASSTEX evaluation idea:** After the first enemy group withdraws, re-evaluate flank threats, separation from supporting elements, cover and mission limits before chasing. Log the reason for pursuit, pause or withdrawal.

### E4 — Prepared fire wins the opening moments: Robert Kotlowitz, 26th Division, Lorraine, 1944

**Situation (reported):** Kotlowitz described a dawn assault across no-man's-land in a platoon diamond formation. As the lead men reached a rise, German machine-gun, mortar, rifle and grenade fire struck. The platoon lieutenant, Francis Gallagher, was shot within roughly ten seconds of cresting the rise; surviving men fell to the ground. Kotlowitz described a catastrophic outcome for his platoon. This was **not** a mutual surprise meeting engagement. [S4]

**What the evidence supports:** A prepared, well-positioned defender may generate a severe initial advantage and suppress/disorganize an attacking platoon quickly.

**What it does not support:** Treating every 50–150 m two-way encounter as a perfect first-shot ambush, or treating a known defensive engagement as an accuracy benchmark for moving patrols.

**GRASSTEX evaluation idea:** Separate "prepared defender already oriented and able to fire" from "two moving parties acquire one another"; report the opening casualty curve alongside shooter readiness, exposure and whether fire-source localization occurred.

### E5 — A cautious street advance, not a squad sprint: Ernie Pyle, Normandy, column dated 21 July 1944

**Situation (observed):** Pyle accompanied U.S. troops heading toward a German strongpoint down a street. Incoming 20 mm fire caused the men to crouch behind a wall. Once it ceased, soldiers crossed an exposed culvert one at a time, moved to both sides of the street, paused, and watched across the street for snipers. The lieutenant repeatedly told them to spread out; Pyle observed a strong tendency to bunch together for reassurance. Grenades, BARs, a bazooka and supporting machine guns/mortars were present in the force. [S5]

**What the evidence supports:** Threat-aware spacing, short exposure windows, mutual observation of flanks, interruptions, and tension between orders and individual behavior.

**What it does not support:** That every urban square is crossed single file, that a street is automatically secure once crossing begins, or that this column records a true instantaneous U.S.–German squad collision.

**GRASSTEX evaluation idea:** Urban contact instrumentation should include sector observations, cross-street danger, building-side threat uncertainty, bunching/cohesion, and transitions between wall cover and street crossing. Evaluate progress, not just idle-versus-moving time.

### E6 — Suppression and edging forward through hedgerows: Ernie Pyle, Normandy, 11 August 1944

**Situation (observed/generalized):** Pyle describes infantry progressing in widely spaced tiny groups, close to field-edge hedges, creeping forward, pausing, and moving again. German machine guns and riflemen were difficult to see behind hedges. Pyle specifically notes that keeping up fire into the defended hedgerow, despite lacking visible individual targets, could keep defenders down while other U.S. troops approached. At close range they used grenades and other weapons at the defended hedge corners; some Germans withdrew to successive positions or surrendered. [S6]

**What the evidence supports:** Suppressive fire against a recently observed/credible area is meaningful without current person-level line of sight; supporting and moving elements can act concurrently; approaching and clearing a defended position is a progression, not simply standing in cover shooting.

**What it does not support:** High hit accuracy through opaque cover, firing through real ballistic obstructions, unlimited target memory, or pretending that a squad-level suppressor has exact live enemy coordinates.

**GRASSTEX evaluation idea:** Differentiate an *observed target* from a *time-stamped reported sector*. Suppression may reduce enemy willingness to expose or fire but must obey ammunition, arcs, genuine ballistic cover, risk, and uncertainty. Log actual covering shots during movement, physical arrivals at successive cover and whether close-range actions actually become possible.

## Comparative interpretation for the first moments of fighting

| Contact context | Source-grounded opening pattern | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Very close unexpected patrol contact | A prepared/alert individual might fire almost immediately (E1) | One remarkable account is not average marksmanship |
| Mutual woodland discovery | Short firefight, uncertain initiator, local automatic fire; possible surrender (E2) | Intent and sequence may be unknowable |
| Contact along a flank | Brief success followed by overextension and isolation (E3) | "Enemy disengaged" is not equivalent to "terrain secured" |
| Cresting exposed ground under a waiting enemy | Immediate heavy defensive fire and casualties (E4) | **Ambush/prepared fire**, not a fair meeting |
| Advancing along a threatened street | Temporary halt behind protection; dispersed, staggered crossings (E5) | Urban approach example, not proof of all first-contact reactions |
| Bocage attack on concealed defense | Area fire, small advances, corner fights and repeated defensive lines (E6) | Platoon/company description, not a universal squad stopwatch |

### The main distinction

**Detection advantage and fire-superiority advantage are related, not identical.** Seeing an enemy first may allow a soldier to act first. It does not imply every subsequent round hits, that the target cannot react, or that a squad with no precise line of sight is unable to contribute area suppression. Conversely, when an enemy is already positioned, oriented and firing from cover, the attacking side can lose the initiative very quickly. These are *qualitative* distinctions from testimony, not measured distributions. [S1] [S4] [S6]

## Focused follow-up: What happened first in a surprise encounter at roughly 50–150 meters?

**Research question (26 October 2026):** If two *unprepared* U.S. and German infantry squads unexpectedly saw each other at approximately 50–150 meters (about 55–164 yards), did the first men stop and shoot, drop for cover, or fire while running?

### Result and confidence

**Best-supported qualitative answer:** There is **no justified historical percentage or single universal response**. The evidence distinguishes *unanswered visual contact* (often observation/holding fire), *contact accompanied by incoming fire* (stop, get down/find cover, orient, fire), and *extremely close confrontation* (sometimes immediate individual shooting or aggressive forward action). Once firing positions and sectors are established, movement and supporting fire become separate but coordinated tasks. The first man may act before the squad leader, and different men can react differently. **Shooting accurately while continuously running is not established as the normal opening behavior** by the reviewed accounts or 1940 U.S. instructions. [S1] [S11] [S12] [S13] [S14] [S15] [S16]

**Confidence:** Moderate for the *conditional distinctions* (supported by explicit wartime U.S. and U.S.-translated German instructional documents plus several eyewitness episodes). **Low for the word "generally"** if it means a measurable majority in genuine *simultaneous*, two-squad meetings at 50–150 m. We did not find a sufficiently large, distance-controlled set of such engagements; many detailed sources concern only one surprised individual, an already prepared ambush, or a deliberate attack. Further, 100 **feet** is only about 30 m, whereas 100 **yards** is about 91 m. Do not merge those observations into the same range cohort.

### Period U.S. doctrine: more specific than "shoot or go prone"

The U.S. War Department's **1940 FM 7-5, *Organization and Tactics of Infantry; the Rifle Battalion*** gives a rare explicit period sequence. Its **paragraph 215(b), printed page 160, PDF page index 164**, addresses scouts *when fired on*: they stop, seek cover, attempt to establish the enemy firing position; if discovered, one scout of a pair moves to a nearby firing position and shoots while the other observes and helps direct fire. **This is what the manual prescribed—not an observed statistical distribution of wartime behavior.** [S17]

The same manual, **paragraph 225, printed page 165, PDF page index 169**, allows very abbreviated fire orders in extreme haste, or initiative by individual riflemen when the squad is exposed. Its following **paragraph 226, printed pages 165–166, PDF indices 169–170**, describes moving by individual, small-group or squad rushes in coordination with one's own or adjacent elements' fire. It specifically distinguishes periods of movement, when supporting fire intensifies, from periods without movement, when ammunition may be conserved. [S17]

**Implication:** The doctrine does not require a whole squad to wait until every rifle has fired before anyone moves, but does describe **fire support and movement as coordinated roles**, not a stream of individual runners making precision shots in motion. It also does not say all men must go prone on sight alone.

### Wartime German perspective: an important qualified counterexample

The U.S. War Department's **Military Intelligence Service, *The German Squad in Combat*, Special Series No. 9 (25 January 1943)** summarizes German training and examples. In its section **"The Infantry Point in Contact with the Enemy," printed pp. 66–67**:

- Against **weak resistance**, the point promptly brings its light machine gun into action and riflemen move to attack using cover and an advantageous approach.
- Against a **stronger enemy**, the point initially seeks cover while support comes up.
- Against an enemy **suddenly firing from under about 100 yards** in terrain providing the enemy cover, the instruction describes an **immediate rush** rather than going to ground to start shooting; the stated rationale is that dropping to begin firing might incur heavy losses at such short range. [S18]

**Important caution:** This last item is a **prescribed German response to a special close-range case**, not direct evidence that all German soldiers actually charged under fire, or that sprinting and shooting accurately were simultaneous actions. "Under 100 yards" is **under ~91 m**, the nearer subset of our 50–150 m question. It also directly conflicts with making prone the mandatory first stance in every short-range meeting.

### Should "fire while moving" be the default?

**No—not from this evidence.** We found support for: (1) hasty shooting from an occupied position (even a hip shot), (2) dropping/ducking for survival, (3) repositioning to cover before or between shots, (4) immediate assault movement in some extremely close situations, and (5) suppression by one element while another moves. Those are **not equivalent** to a game soldier running and delivering accurate continuous fire at another running soldier. The records do not establish a share of all engagements where moving fire occurred, so this is an evidence limit rather than an assertion that it never happened.

### Game behavior hypotheses and a falsifiable test

For the **480×360 m equal-force meeting scenario** already used by GRASSTEX, compare **three explicitly labeled observation conditions**, *without changing any weapon or tactical tuning in this document*:

| Observation at first cue | Plausible behavior to evaluate (not a mandated rule) | Evidence to capture |
|---|---|---|
| **One squad sees unidentified figures, neither side fires** | Some observers stop/slow to identify, orient, report, or hold fire; others follow mission and training | Sighted-vs-identified timestamps, shooter confidence, held shots, false-positive/friendly risk [S12] [S16] |
| **Enemy opens fire on moving squad** | Exposed men seek protection and may return hasty fire; others acquire sectors or take firing positions. Responses need not be simultaneous | Per-soldier cue-to-cover, cue-to-first-actual-shot, contact-report age, physically reached cover and fire geometry [S14] [S17] |
| **Both groups recognize hostile opponents almost together** | A few alert men may fire immediately, others go to ground/cover or attempt to reposition; no universal enforced team synchronization | Which actor fired first, how many fired while stationary vs while moving, hit/miss, command adoption, cohesion, casualties in opening window [S1] [S11] [S13] |

**Instrumentation:** For every shot inside a short analysis window after first contact, record shooter velocity, stance, aim state, weapon type/readiness, target visibility, target velocity, angular exposure, cover/intervening geometry, hit outcome and whether a movement order was active. For each soldier record exact first cue, actual ground/cover response, first fired round and completed movement (not just requests). Analyze by **measured initial distance**, with separate bins for **<50 m**, **50–100 m**, and **100–150 m**, and split **mutual discovery / first-seen-undetected / receiving surprise fire**. Don't create a single combined average.

**Acceptance criteria for the research hypothesis:** The simulation should demonstrate **meaningfully distinct outcomes** for the three encounter types, with no imposed universal prone pause, no omniscient synchronized detection, no disguised through-cover precision shots, and no claim that rapid movement *must* be paired with accurate running fire. Any actual mechanics still require their own PR, controller-owner checks and native live-enemy QA.

### Open historical question

We **still cannot say "most men did X" with a defensible frequency** at exactly 50–150 meters in a balanced, mutual, unprepared squad meeting. The high-value next source would be multiple independently recorded U.S. **and** German platoon patrol after-action interviews that note both sides' discovery circumstances, approximate initial distance, first response and who initiated the shooting. Until that exists, a **conditional model validated against multiple field accounts** is more faithful than inventing a "70% prone, 20% shoot, 10% run" reaction table.


## McRaven: *Spec Ops* (1995/1996), theory and WWII case studies

**Work:** William H. McRaven, *Spec Ops: Case Studies in Special Operations Warfare: Theory and Practice*, Presidio Press (original edition 1995; 1996 paperback; later editions). The publisher and contemporary review identify eight major studies, **six of them WWII operations**: the 1940 German assault on Eben Emael; the 1941 Italian manned-torpedo attack in Alexandria harbor; the 1942 British Saint-Nazaire raid; the 1943 German rescue of Mussolini at Gran Sasso; the 1943 British midget-submarine attack on *Tirpitz*; and the U.S./Filipino Cabanatuan prisoner rescue in 1945. The other two concern Son Tay (1970) and Entebbe (1976). [S7] [S8]

### Central concept: temporary relative superiority

McRaven's framework concerns how a *small, purpose-built assault force* can generate a decisive, sometimes brief advantage against a larger or heavily defended opponent. The six principles named by the publisher are:

| McRaven principle | Reasonable **game-design interpretation** | Transfer limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Simplicity** | A squad plan has a clear near-term objective and few ambiguous competing tasks | This is not evidence that every planned action must be trivial |
| **Security** | Preserve surprise / information advantage by reducing premature exposure of intent | Chance meetings typically lack prepared secrecy |
| **Repetition** | Experienced/rehearsed unit executes familiar team tasks with fewer coordination failures | Do not grant an improvised squad a rehearsed-raid bonus |
| **Surprise** | A defender who has not detected the attackers cannot pre-orient instantly | Must derive from actual perception, not omniscient scenario flags |
| **Speed** | Once an opportunity appears, timely execution matters before the opponent responds | Speed is not merely high running velocity or an artificially accelerated clock |
| **Purpose** | Units remain directed toward the committed local task and adapt when it changes | Purpose does not justify suicidal persistence or ignoring new threats |

**Boundary:** These are interpretations intended to inspire game design, **not** direct quotations, precise definitions from inaccessible pages, or empirically calibrated values. The six principle names and case-study roster are verified by the publisher and reviews. [S7] [S8]

### WWII example A — Eben Emael, 10 May 1940

Historical U.S. Army studies describe German glider troops arriving onto the Belgian fort's roof in an unexpected direction, disabling key positions despite two gliders missing the opening assault—including the commander's glider. A senior NCO carried on the prepared plan. The combination of preparation, unusual approach, local leadership and surprise helps explain why an initially small force achieved a disproportionate result. [S9]

**Game-design implication:** A well-rehearsed task can remain coherent when a leader is unavailable, if subordinate actors possess valid prior intent. Do *not* transfer this effect wholesale to surprised, unprepared conventional infantry.

### WWII example B — Cabanatuan rescue, 30 January 1945

U.S. Army histories describe Rangers, Alamo Scouts and Filipino guerrillas coordinating deep reconnaissance, approach, supporting actions and prisoner extraction; roughly 500-plus prisoners were rescued. The case demonstrates that a planned mission's **completion** includes exit, protection and accounting for vulnerable people—not just the opening assault. [S10]

**Game-design implication:** In scripted or mission-oriented scenarios, distinguish "seized initial advantage" from "secured the mission and successfully extracted / consolidated." A regular encounter should likewise distinguish winning contact from holding or safely advancing through the area.

### Explicit non-transfer rule

Do **not** conflate (a) special-operations attackers who know their objective, rehearse and arrange surprise; (b) ordinary infantry deliberately attacking a suspected defense; and (c) two patrols encountering one another without preparation. McRaven is valuable for **momentary advantage, mission coherence and exploitation**, not as proof that ordinary U.S.–German rifle squads should always rapidly wipe one another out or execute rehearsed raids.

### Contact-state instrumentation worth testing

Record these as independent timestamped events rather than inventing a universal "reaction delay":

- **First observable cue:** visual acquisition, incoming shot, impact / sound cue, or reported sector; actor and confidence.
- **Soldier response:** first look/orient, lower profile/cover request, first return shot, change of immediate task and actual movement start.
- **Squad response:** first contact report, leader understanding, plan adoption, assignment of support/movement roles, change after leader loss.
- **Fire quality and effect:** identified-target shots vs area/sector fire; rounds physically fired; target movement, exposure, ballistic obstruction, outcomes and sustained enemy response.
- **Movement:** ordered destination vs actual start, traversed path, real cover arrival, ability to fire from the reached position, and further movement after the first contact.
- **Outcome:** first wound, first casualty, first surrender/withdrawal, local advantage gained/lost, squad cohesion and objective control (all distinct).

### Minimal comparison matrix

| Scenario | Perturbation | What to inspect |
|---|---|---|
| Open-field meeting, both squads mobile | Equal initial information, varied first detection | Initiative vs hit/miss/cover reaction; is "who sees first wins" inevitable? |
| Prepared defenders vs advancing attackers | Defender ready, covered, oriented | Does high initial lethality occur in this specific context rather than all contexts? |
| Dense hedgerow, unseen defenders | Recent contact report but no direct target LOS | Real sector suppression vs fabricated precision kills; bounding to next real cover |
| Woodland chance encounter | Limited LOS and intersecting patrol paths | Individual first actions, command uncertainty, formation disruption, disengagement |
| Urban intersection and buildings | Exposed crossing and several plausible threat sectors | Wall use, flank coverage, spacing, incremental movement, stale threat localization |
| Pursuit after local success | Enemy falls back, possible second threat/flank | Overextension, isolation, stop/reassess, cover, safe consolidation |
| Preplanned scripted raid (separate mode) | Clear target and rehearsed role allocation | Relative advantage window; resilience to leader loss; mission completion beyond entry |

### Questions to answer with measurements

1. Does the current weapon model let accurate shooters apply hit outcomes instantly at discovery, with no target travel/exposure interval? If so, how much of contact lethality is ballistic modeling vs AI choice? **Not established by the historical accounts.**
2. Are soldiers slow because their *movement orders* are delayed, because they are under realistic suppressive pressure, or because requests do not result in actual owner-executed movement? Distinguish each stage.
3. Do sector reports permit non-precision suppression at plausible locations while preserving true line-of-fire obstruction and uncertainty?
4. Do squads lose the chance to maneuver because covering fire is absent after the opening exchange, even though many weapons continue firing?
5. Are near-contact assaults/grenade opportunities earned through actual physical movement, and are grenade safety decisions continuously valid as friendlies move?
6. When a local opponent withdraws or dies, does the victorious squad continue purposeful maneuver/security instead of entering a permanent stationary posture?

**PR context:** At the time this note was written, [draft #679](https://github.com/APPARANYX/grasstex/pull/679) reported native 480×360 m meeting-battle trials that passed certain mechanical checks but recorded **zero covering-fire events and zero grenade throws** in those two runs. That is not proof the desired natural meeting-battle doctrine is achieved; check the current PR head and new evidence before using the statement as present-state truth.

### Anti-patterns to avoid

- Tuning everyone to miss merely because one anecdote describes confusion.
- Tuning everyone to hit because Ehlers recalled four quick hits.
- Treating all battles as ambushes or all contacts as equally prepared.
- Using a stale contact sector as exact current enemy truth, or allowing rounds through physical cover.
- Forcing all soldiers to wait for a central squad timer before making urgent individual survival decisions.
- Counting nominal "support assigned" as actual suppressive shots; counting issued movement as completed movement.
- Making an objective-claimed/defend phase halt local repositioning or flank security.

## Research gaps for a subsequent pass

- Direct German patrol/infantry firsthand accounts to counter U.S.-centered survivor bias.
- More genuine *mutual* discovery engagements at comparable ranges; most available dramatic accounts are ambushes, deliberate assaults or exceptional individual episodes.
- Period after-action reports that separately record detection, first fire, movement and casualties with reliable time resolution.
- Full primary-text read of McRaven, especially his formal definition of relative superiority and his analysis of the relationship between preparation, surprise and the decisive point; preserve chapter/page citations when accessible legally.
- Representative enough sources to distinguish veteran recollection from tactical base rates. Until then, treat all proposed timing or hit-rate changes as **hypotheses to validate through simulator probes and gameplay observation**.
